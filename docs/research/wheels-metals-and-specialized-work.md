## Node proposal

Essential question (provisional): Why did wheels and metal tools need so many people, places and animals before they could change anything?
Durable understanding (provisional): Between about 3600 and 2000 BCE, wheels and metals spread across western Eurasia as systems of materials, animals and specialist skill rather than as single inventions, and those who controlled the systems gained most from them.
Supporting understandings:
1. Metal came from particular places and moved with people; a single copper axe could link communities hundreds of kilometres apart.
2. Metalworking was a sequence of specialist tasks, and harder alloys (arsenical copper, then tin bronze) needed materials that were often not local.
3. Wheeled vehicles appeared across a wide region within a few centuries; the evidence does not identify one inventor or one starting place.
4. A wheel was useful only with skilled woodworking, trained draft animals and suitable routes, which is why knowing about wheels was not the same as using carts.
5. In early states such as Ur, these technologies concentrated in the hands of rulers and depended on long-distance supply.
Evidence encounter: the Ljubljana Marshes wheel and axle (MGML 510:LJU;0035518–9), with Ötzi's copper axe as the opening object; the Standard of Ur (BM 121201) if beat 5 is kept.
Prerequisites: lesson.farming.settlements (villages, herded cattle, surplus). Canonical position 9, lesson.animals.domestication-and-pastoralism, is unpublished; this lesson will not assume it and will explain draft oxen itself.
Common misconceptions: "the wheel was invented once, in Mesopotamia"; "the Bronze Age began everywhere at the same time"; "an invention spreads because it is obviously better"; "the potter's wheel and the cart wheel are the same story"; "every society that knew about wheels should have used carts".
Scope — dates/places/actors: c. 3600–2000 BCE (Ur at c. 2550–2400 BCE closes the lesson); Alps, Ljubljana Marshes, northern Europe, Carpathian Basin, Anatolia (Arslantepe), Mesopotamia (Uruk, Ur); the Balkans c. 5000 BCE only as the earlier metallurgical background; ancient Mexico (c. 600–900 CE) only as one comparison.
Why this is one lesson: wheels and metals arrive in the same centuries, need the same kind of answer (a system of specialists, materials and animals), and together explain what early cities would organize.
Non-goals/deferred material: horses, spoked wheels and chariots (Steppe Mobility and the Chariot); the full Bronze Age trade web, tin routes and Uluburun (Bronze Age Exchange Networks); Shang bronze casting (Shang Power, Bronze, and Oracle Bones); iron (Iron, Farming, and New States); the full Varna gold cemetery; Ötzi's death; detailed alloy chemistry.
Bridge from previous lesson: retrieve Farming and Settlements' herded cattle and village surplus: the same cattle, castrated and trained, could now pull a cart.
Bridge to next lesson: Uruk: Life in an Early City shows a city coordinating many kinds of specialist work.
Geographic orientation: Europe and the Middle East → the Alps, the Carpathian Basin, Anatolia and Mesopotamia → Ljubljana Marshes, Flintbek, Arslantepe, Uruk and Ur.
Visible native orientation sentence and intended map size (provisional): "Within a few centuries, wheels appear from northern Germany to southern Iraq." Full-width embedded map, legible on mobile.

## Research questions

- When and where does the earliest secure evidence for wheeled vehicles appear, and how reliable are those dates?
- Is there a single origin for the wheel, and what is the current status of the Mesopotamian, Pontic, Carpathian and polycentric proposals?
- What did a working wheel require (materials, joinery, animals, routes)?
- Where and when did copper smelting begin, and was it invented once or several times?
- How did arsenical copper and tin bronze differ, and where did their ingredients come from?
- What does a single object (Ötzi's axe) show about how metal moved?
- Which specialists can be shown from evidence rather than assumed (wheelwright, smith, miner)?
- Who gained from these technologies, and whose work is poorly preserved?
- Which comparisons (the Americas, East Asia) clarify that a technology needs a system without ranking societies?
- What stories did ancient people themselves tell about copper and metal tools?

## Learning blueprint

Essential question: Why did wheels and metal tools need so many people, places and animals before they could change anything?
Durable understanding: Between about 3600 and 2000 BCE, wheels and metals spread across Europe and western Asia as systems of materials, trained animals and specialist skill rather than as single inventions, and rulers gathered many of their products.
Supporting understandings: (1) Metal came from particular places and moved with people. (2) Metalworking was a chain of skilled tasks, and harder alloys needed distant materials such as tin. (3) Wheeled vehicles appeared across a wide region within a few centuries; the first place is unknown. (4) A wheel was useful only with skilled woodworking, trained animals and routes. (5) Early cities like Ur gathered the products of these systems, mostly for the powerful.
Prerequisites: villages and herded cattle (lesson.farming.settlements); a sense of where Europe, Anatolia and Mesopotamia are; the idea that dates come with margins.
Misconceptions: the wheel was invented once in Mesopotamia; the Bronze Age began everywhere at once; inventions spread because they are obviously better; every society that knew the wheel should have used carts.
Indispensable vocabulary: ore, smelting, alloy, bronze, axle, oxen, wheelwright.
Evidence encounter: the Ljubljana Marshes wheel and axle (MGML 510:LJU;0035518–9).
Historical-thinking move: corroborate several dated finds to reach a qualified conclusion (no single first place), then explain a technology as a system.
Retrieve: herded cattle, stored food and village life from lesson.farming.settlements (the same cattle, castrated and trained, could now pull a cart).
Extend: farming villages now support specialists such as smiths and wheelwrights, and depend on materials from far away.
Revisit: lesson.uruk.first-city (city coordination of specialist work, published); lesson.bronze-age.exchange-networks (tin and long-distance trade, planned); lesson.steppe.chariots-and-mobility (spoked wheels and horses, planned); lesson.china.shang-bronze-and-writing (another bronze tradition, planned).
Reasoning progression: earlier lessons practiced reading one object closely → this lesson compares dated evidence from several places to qualify a conclusion → later lessons (Exchange Networks) weigh provenance evidence such as isotopes more independently.
Transfer plan: the Veracruz comparison is a light, unfamiliar case reasoned in the prose; a full transfer task is deferred to Bronze Age Exchange Networks, where tin-provenance evidence can be judged independently.
Completion versus mastery: completion records reading, the right wheel-evidence choice after retries and a sincere written attempt; mastery would show if a learner could later explain an unfamiliar technology (for example the chariot) as a system without prompting.
Required sincere-attempt evidence: the written answer naming two needs of the axe or wheel and the people involved.
Story spine: Ötzi’s axe opens the lesson; the Ljubljana wheel is the evidence encounter; Ur brings both together, and the closing paragraph calls back to the axe.
Memorable moments: copper from Tuscany in an Alpine axe; the 5,000-year-old wheel mistaken for a barrel lid and part-sliced by a digger; the repair wedge; kunga hybrids pulling Ur’s wagons; Copper boasting against Silver.

## Ages 11–15 transformations

Initial drafting reference: roughly 12–13; age fit remains a hypothesis until observed with learners.

- Lead-isotope provenance → “lead from each ore deposit carries its own chemical signature.”
- Calibrated radiocarbon ranges and Bayesian models → “dates from this far back carry margins of a century or two.”
- Polycentric vs monocentric debate → “nobody can yet say which place came first”, plus three named ideas in one sentence.
- Arsenical copper and tin-bronze percentages → “copper mixed with arsenic”, “copper mixed with tin”; no percentages in learner text.
- Wood-science detail (radial planks, shrinkage percentages) → “avoided the weak wood at the centre”, “swelled in rain and shrank in sun.”
- Castration → stated plainly once with its purpose (calmer, trainable), no further detail.
- Ötzi’s death → omitted; the find is described, not the cause of death.
- Veracruz comparison → framed as what a wheel needs, never as a society that “failed.”
