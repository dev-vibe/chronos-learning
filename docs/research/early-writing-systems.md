# Early Writing Systems research and editorial note

Reviewed 2026-07-16 for ASH-57. Repository content remains canonical; the database stores only publication, prompt, completion, and deterministic unlock configuration.

## Learner-facing scope

The stable lesson ID remains `lesson.writing.early-systems` and the legacy alias remains `sumer_writing`. The learner-facing title is **From Marks to Proto-Cuneiform** because the evidence in this increment is specifically Mesopotamian and concentrated on late-fourth-millennium administrative practices. Calling the lesson simply "Early Writing Systems" could imply coverage of every early writing tradition or a single universal origin. The lesson instead names other independent traditions briefly and does not claim descent from Mesopotamia.

Approximate chronology: c. 3200-2800 BCE. Journey position: immediately after `lesson.uruk.first-city` in World History / Foundations.

## Reviewed source basis

- [The Met, Proto-Cuneiform tablet with seal impressions, 1988.433.1](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/329081): object record, date, probable Uruk provenance, material, dimensions, public-domain image status, and the museum's cautious interpretation as a probable barley-distribution account.
- [The Met, The Origins of Writing](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing): institutional orientation for the shift from pictographic marks toward later cuneiform.
- [CDLI, W 20498 / P003801](https://cdli.earth/P003801): specialist corpus comparison for an Uruk IV administrative tablet; used as research context, not as redistributed media.
- [CDLI, The Origins of Writing as a Problem of Historical Epistemology](https://cdli.earth/articles/cdlj/2006-1): specialist discussion of what proto-cuneiform can represent and the limits of treating it as equivalent to later language-rich cuneiform.
- [Kelley, Cartolano, and Ferrara, "Seals and signs," Antiquity](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804): peer-reviewed treatment of seals, tokens, numerical practices, and possible sign precursors; explicitly notes that simple token-to-sign correspondences are rarely demonstrable.
- [ISAC, Visible Language](https://isac.uchicago.edu/programs-events/member-events/visible-language-inventions-writing-ancient-middle-east-and-beyond): institutional context for independently developing writing traditions.

## Editorial distinctions

- **Surviving evidence:** clay tablets, inscribed or impressed marks, numerical signs, and seal impressions.
- **Scholarly interpretation:** readings of commodities, quantities, institutional activity, and probable transactions derived by comparing objects, signs, find contexts, and later texts.
- **Uncertainty:** the exact transaction on the featured Met tablet is not certain; the object lacks verbs, many early signs remain disputed, and individual token-to-sign or seal-to-sign paths are not assumed.
- **Later cuneiform:** later wedge-impressed cuneiform and its capacity to record multiple spoken languages are described as developments over time, not properties projected backward onto every proto-cuneiform sign.
- **Archive limits:** durable administrative tablets are a surviving sample, not a complete record of writing's uses or everyone's experience.
- **Wider context:** Mesopotamian proto-cuneiform is not presented as the ancestor of every writing system.
