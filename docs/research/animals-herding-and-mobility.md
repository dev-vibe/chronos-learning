## Node proposal

Essential question: How did people turn wild animals into herds, and why did living with herds become its own way of life?
Durable understanding: People in several regions turned hunted animals into managed herds by controlling which animals lived and bred; herding, often on the move, became a skilled and lasting way of life that sat beside farming and connected distant communities.
Supporting understandings: (1) Domestication began with human management, visible in bones before bodies changed. (2) It happened independently or separately in several regions with different animals. (3) Live animals supplied milk and later wool and pulling power, at different times, not as one package. (4) In much of Africa herding preceded crop farming and supported large shared monuments. (5) Mobility was a strategy for uncertain water and grass, and herders depended on and traded with farmers and each other.
Evidence encounter: Ganj Dareh goat age-and-sex profile (Daly et al. 2021 Fig. 2A, CC BY 4.0) contrasted with the hunted profile at Asiab; supporting chemical evidence of milk in pottery.
Prerequisites: domestication and farming from `lesson.farming.multiple-origins`; mixed farming at Boncuklu/Çatalhöyük from `lesson.farming.settlements`.
Common misconceptions: domestication was a single moment of taming in one village; herders were "primitive" people at a stage between hunting and farming; nomads wander without plan; animals were always kept mainly for meat; milk drinking needs adult lactose digestion; farming always came first.
Scope — dates/places/actors: c. 9000–2000 BCE; Zagros and Fertile Crescent, Indus borderlands, Andes puna, Sahara and Lake Turkana, northwest Arabia, the Pontic-Caspian steppe as bridge only; herders, farmers, and the animals.
Why this is one lesson: one mechanism (management of animal life and movement) organizes every case; regional cases test it rather than being a catalogue.
Non-goals/deferred material: horse domestication, riding and chariots (Spine 20); carts and ploughs (Spine 10); modern pastoralism except as clearly labelled present-day comparison; dog domestication; Mari tribal politics (later; analogy only in research).
Bridge from previous lesson: Boncuklu kept a few sheep and goats while still foraging — how did those animals become herds, and did everyone settle down with them?
Bridge to next lesson: animals that carry, pull and give milk set up wheels, carts and specialized work.
Geographic orientation: Africa, Asia and South America on a world map → highlighted dry or mountain regions → named sites (Ganj Dareh, Takarkori, Lothagam North, Telarmachay).
Visible native orientation sentence and intended map size: "Herding began in several places: the Zagros Mountains of Iran, the Indus borderlands, the Sahara and East Africa, and the high Andes of South America." One full-width world map.

## Research questions

- What evidence distinguishes a managed herd from a hunted population, and how early is it?
- Was animal domestication one event or many? Which animals, where, roughly when?
- When did people start using milk, wool and pulling power, and did these arrive together?
- Why did herding precede plant farming in much of Africa, and what did early herders build?
- Were African cattle domesticated separately? What is the current evidence on each side?
- Why do pastoralists move, and how did mobile herders relate to farmers and to each other?
- What role did dairying play in later steppe mobility, without stealing the chariot lesson?
- Which inherited stories (stage model, secondary-products "revolution", primitive nomads) need correcting?
- Which visuals teach the mechanism honestly, and which are openly licensed?

## Recent-challenge audit (Stages 3A–3B)

Baseline account that would otherwise be inherited: animals were "tamed" in one Neolithic heartland, mostly for meat; herding was a stage between hunting and farming; a "secondary products revolution" in the 4th millennium BCE suddenly added milk, wool and traction; nomads were marginal wanderers dependent on settled farmers; Africa received herding and farming from outside as one package.
Default search window: approximately 1976–2026. Extended back to Sherratt 1981/1983 because it is the baseline being tested.

## Learning blueprint

Essential question: How did people turn wild animals into herds, and why did living with herds become its own way of life?
Durable understanding: People in several regions turned hunted animals into managed herds by controlling which animals lived and bred; herding, often on the move, became a skilled and lasting way of life alongside farming.
Supporting understandings: (1) Bones show management before bodies changed. (2) Several regions domesticated different animals. (3) Live animals gave milk, and later pulling power and wool, at different times. (4) In Africa herding came before crops, and herders built monuments together. (5) Mobility was a strategy for uncertain water and grass, and herders stayed connected to others.
Prerequisites: domestication as a slow process (lesson.farming.multiple-origins); mixed farming with a few sheep and goats at Boncuklu (lesson.farming.settlements).
Misconceptions: domestication as one moment of taming in one village; herders as a primitive stage; nomads as aimless wanderers; animals kept only for meat; milk drinking requires adult digestion; farming always came first.
Indispensable vocabulary: herd/herding, domestication, aurochs, camelid, dairying, mobility (pastoralism is avoided in learner text in favour of "herding").
Evidence encounter: Ganj Dareh goat age-and-sex pattern compared with Asiab, plus the hoofprinted brick.
Historical-thinking move: comparing two evidence patterns (hunted vs. herded) to infer human choices, then qualifying what the bones cannot show.
Retrieve: from lesson.farming.multiple-origins, domestication as gradual inherited change driven by ordinary choices (the wheat-ear mechanism); from lesson.farming.settlements, Boncuklu keeping a few sheep and goats while foraging.
Extend: the same gradual-selection logic applied to animals, with the new twist that people controlled which animals bred and that herding could mean leaving villages rather than settling.
Revisit: planned in lesson.technology.wheels-metals-and-work (animal pulling power, carts) and lesson.steppe.chariots-and-mobility (steppe milk, horses); both are unpublished and named here as editorial intentions only.
Reasoning progression: prior lessons practiced observing plant evidence and naming its limits → here the learner compares two bone patterns to infer a human choice → next, Wheels and Steppe lessons ask learners to weigh several evidence types for a technology's spread.
Transfer plan: no unfamiliar-evidence transfer task in this lesson; the Andean corrals give a light second case of management evidence, and fuller transfer is deferred to the Steppe lesson, where horse-domestication evidence can be tested with the same "management vs. hunting" logic.
Completion versus mastery: completion records the correct Ganj Dareh selection and a sincere written attempt on mobility; mastery would be shown if a learner could use the hunted-vs-herded logic on new bones (for example camelids) without prompting, which is not tested here.
Required sincere-attempt evidence: two separate written answers, one explaining a reason for moving (minimum 30 characters) and one posing an open question (minimum 15 characters).
Story spine: the hoofprinted bricks of Ganj Dareh open the lesson; the herd idea travels across regions to the Sahara and Lake Turkana, and closes on herders who move by knowledge, not chance.
Memorable moments: hoofprints pressed into a mud brick 10,000 years ago; goat bones that reveal herders' choices; people using milk thousands of years before most adults could digest it; at least 580 people buried together by early herders at Lake Turkana.
