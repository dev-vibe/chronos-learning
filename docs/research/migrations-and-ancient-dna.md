### Title rationale

The canonical title stays unchanged. **Migrations** is plural because movements happened at different times and some early lineages left little later ancestry. **Encounters** keeps people, not genes, at the center. **Ancient DNA** names the evidence and creates room to teach its limits. “Out of Africa,” “replacement,” and “conquest” would turn a branching population history into a single triumphant march.

## Curriculum boundary

### In scope

- Repeated Homo sapiens dispersals from Africa, with the expansion ancestral to most living non-African populations beginning roughly 50,000 years ago.
- Neanderthal and Denisovan populations already present in Eurasia.
- Shared and additional episodes of interbreeding, including Oase 1.
- Recombination shortening inherited DNA segments over generations.
- Denisovan ancestry as evidence for more than one encounter in Asia and Oceania.
- Ancient-DNA preservation, sampling, inference, destructive analysis, and identity limits.

### Explicitly deferred

- The open-water crossing to Sahul; that is the successor lesson.
- Detailed peopling of Europe or Asia, later Ice Age lifeways, and the peopling of the Americas.
- A single-cause explanation for Neanderthal disappearance.
- Taxonomic debates over species versus subspecies.
- Trait catalogues or claims that genes determine culture, intelligence, ethnicity, or present-day identity.
- Denisova 11, the child of a Neanderthal mother and Denisovan father. It is important but not required to establish this lesson’s Homo sapiens encounter story.

## Research questions

1. Which claims distinguish repeated movements from one migration event?
2. What does current evidence support about the timing and recurrence of Neanderthal admixture?
3. What makes Oase 1 legible without making one person represent everyone?
4. What can Denisovan ancestry show when the fossil record is tiny?
5. Which map conventions would create false certainty?
6. How can the lesson teach method limits and research ethics without making ancient DNA feel useless?
7. Which language avoids biological ladders, genetic purity, and identity essentialism?

### How disagreement is handled

- **Timing:** use approximate ranges from radiocarbon and segment models.
- **Routes:** evidence sites do not trace a route; the map has no migration arrows.
- **Taxonomy:** population labels are useful but do not settle species/subspecies debates.
- **Tool makers:** do not automatically assign a stone-tool tradition to one population.
- **Denisovan range:** ancestry outside Siberia does not authorize a continuous territory.
- **Identity:** DNA is evidence about ancestry, not an arbiter of culture or belonging.

## Content triage

### Must teach

1. Leaving Africa was a branching history of movements, not one procession.
2. Eurasia already held other human populations.
3. Shared and additional inherited DNA segments record encounters and children.
4. Oase 1 makes “recent ancestor” concrete while showing one lineage need not dominate later ancestry.
5. Denisovan evidence widens the story eastward but remains fragmentary.
6. Ancient DNA is powerful, preservation-biased, model-dependent, destructive to sample, and unable to define identity.

### Supporting detail

- A wider African ecological niche may have helped some populations expand.
- Present-day non-African genomes often contain roughly 2–3% Neanderthal ancestry, but a percentage must never become a purity score.
- Denisovan-related DNA contributing to Tibetan high-altitude adaptation is one downstream example.

### Reject from runtime

- Exact route arrows, universally settled named waves, or territory shading.
- Reconstructions of an intimate encounter or family scene not grounded in evidence.
- “Primitive,” “more evolved,” “caveman,” or “racial ancestor” language.
- A collectible object made from a photographed human bone.
- Quiz recall of ancestry percentages.

## Learning blueprint

### Essential question

When some Homo sapiens moved beyond Africa, did they simply replace everyone they met—or did those encounters become part of human ancestry?

### Durable understanding

People moved out of Africa in more than one dispersal. The expansion ancestral to most living people outside Africa met and had children with Neanderthals and Denisovans. Ancient DNA preserves traces of those encounters, but the sample is incomplete and cannot tell us people’s identities, languages, or exact routes.

### Supporting understandings

1. Ancestry is a branching network, not a ladder of better and worse humans.
2. Long inherited DNA segments point to a more recent ancestor; recombination shortens segments over generations.
3. A sampled person can preserve an encounter even if their lineage contributed little to later populations.
4. Sparse Denisovan fossils and widespread related ancestry show both genetic evidence’s power and its limits.
5. Evidence methods have ethical costs and interpretive boundaries.

### Misconceptions to correct

- “All Homo sapiens left Africa once in a single group.”
- “Neanderthals vanished without descendants.”
- “Neanderthals and Denisovans were failed steps below us.”
- “A DNA percentage determines identity.”
- “Map dots show everywhere people lived.”
- “Genomes reveal what ancient people called themselves.”

## Ages 11–14 design pass

- Open with a replacement-versus-encounter question and complicate it with evidence.
- Introduce no more than three population names in the main narrative, using short sentences and immediate context for unfamiliar terms.
- Explain recombination with one comparison: long inherited segments versus shorter pieces after many generations.
- Use Oase 1, Denisova Cave, and Ranis without naming every genome.
- Define **ancient DNA**, **ancestor**, **population**, and **interbreeding** in context; keep “admixture” in provenance.
- Treat remains as people, not treasure; favor a map and diagram over specimen spectacle.
- Every uncertainty statement answers “what is missing?”
- The final response accepts ordinary language; scientific vocabulary is not required.

## Understanding-check plan

### Prompt 1 — supported selection

- ID: `prompt.humans.long-segments-inference`
- Question: **Oase 1 carried unusually long stretches of Neanderthal DNA. Which conclusion is best supported?**
- Supported: `option.humans.long-segments-recent` - The long pieces point to a recent Neanderthal ancestor.
- Distractors: Oase 1 was Neanderthal; DNA reveals their language; every group had the same encounter.
- Feedback: long segments had fewer generations to be broken into shorter pieces. They support a recent ancestor, not a complete identity or universal history.
- Completion: attempt required; correctness not required.

### Prompt 2 — concise explanation

- ID: `prompt.humans.adna-evidence-and-limit`
- Question: **Use one example to explain what ancient DNA reveals about an encounter. Then name one thing the DNA cannot tell us by itself.**
- Minimum: 30 characters.
- Accepted evidence: shared Neanderthal interval; Oase 1; Denisovan-related ancestry and multiple contacts.
- Accepted limits: exact route, language, culture, motives, identity, complete range, unsampled people, or certainty about a relationship.
- Completion: any sincere in-scope attempt counts; specialist vocabulary is unnecessary.

## Disagreement, uncertainty, and missing voices

- Ancient genomes represent rare places and remains where DNA survived and researchers sampled; Africa and tropical Asia are underrepresented.
- Genetic labels are made by researchers and may not match ancient self-understanding.
- Sampling can be destructive. Copy must mention responsibility to descendant, local, and Indigenous communities without claiming one universal process.
- Living Papuan and Tibetan communities are contemporary peoples, not stand-ins for Denisovans.
- Archaeology, fossils, environment, and community knowledge remain necessary; genetics does not outrank all evidence.
- The voices of the people studied cannot be recovered from DNA. Name that silence; do not invent dialogue.
