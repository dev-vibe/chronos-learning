# Chronos Journey portfolio coverage audit

Date: 2026-08-08
Linear: [Audit the draft Journey roster for coverage gaps and distortions](https://linear.app/ashs-workshop/issue/ASH-78/audit-the-draft-journey-roster-for-coverage-gaps-and-distortions)
Wayfinder map: [Define the complete Chronos journey portfolio](https://linear.app/ashs-workshop/issue/ASH-75/define-the-complete-chronos-journey-portfolio)
Roster audited: [Chronos candidate journey roster](../content/journey-candidate-roster.md)
Status: **Research complete; portfolio decisions remain open**

## Question and boundary

Where does the existing 90-candidate Journey roster overconcentrate, omit, flatten, or duplicate global history, philosophy, ordinary life, historical reasoning, periods, regions, communities, and evidence traditions?

This is a portfolio audit, not a replacement roster. It does **not** decide the final count, categories, titles, production order, or whether any gap should become its own Journey rather than be repaired inside another Journey. It treats Journey overlap as potentially valuable: the same subject may support different Lessons and different Journeys when the learning purpose is genuinely different.

## Method

Every candidate row was reviewed for its stated question, chronological and geographic scope, likely shared anchors, new-content demand, evidence packet, and anti-distortion gate. The audit then compared the portfolio with first-party curriculum and disciplinary frameworks. Counts are diagnostic rather than quotas.

The framework baseline is deliberately plural:

| Source | What it contributes | Limit in this audit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UCLA/National Center for History in the Schools, [World History for Us All: foundations](https://whfua.history.ucla.edu/shared/foundations.php) and [questions and themes](https://whfua.history.ucla.edu/shared/themes.php) | A whole-world, Paleolithic-to-present frame; multiple temporal and spatial scales; comparison; inclusion of privileged and unprivileged people; environment, population, exchange, power, inequality, identity, science/technology, and spiritual or moral life | A framework, not a required content list; some materials are older |
| National Council for the Social Studies, [C3 Framework](https://www.socialstudies.org/sites/default/files/c3/C3-Framework-for-Social-Studies.pdf) | Inquiry through questions, disciplinary tools, sources and evidence, claims, conclusions, and action; historical concepts include change, continuity, context, perspectives, sources, causation, and argumentation | Specifies competencies more than world-history coverage |
| American Historical Association, [History Discipline Core](https://www.historians.org/resource/history-discipline-core/) and [Five Cs of Historical Thinking](https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/what-does-it-mean-to-think-historically-january-2007/) | Breadth and depth; particular past lives; artifacts, written evidence, and oral traditions; complexity, contingency, provisional knowledge, conflicting perspectives, and revision | Written for higher education, so it informs quality rather than age-level scope |
| College Board, [AP World History: Modern course framework](https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-world-history) | A cross-check for modern global themes and reasoning: environment, cultural developments, governance, economic systems, social organization, technology, sourcing, comparison, causation, continuity/change, and argument | Begins about 1200 CE and is exam-oriented; it cannot govern Chronos's deep-time or ages 10–14 design |
| Australian Curriculum, [Humanities and Social Sciences: History](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-learning-area/humanities-and-social-sciences) and [Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Histories and Cultures](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/curriculum-information/understand-this-cross-curriculum-priority/aboriginal-and-torres-strait-islander-histories-and-cultures) | Earliest communities to the present; local-to-global scales; evidence, perspective, contestability, and significance; explicit deep-time and continuing First Nations Australian history | A national curriculum; its Australia requirements are evidence of a real global-history blind spot, not a mandate to copy its sequence |
| UNESCO, [General History of Africa curriculum pathway](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/mainstreaming-general-history-africa-education-systems-curriculum-pathway) | Direct warning against Eurocentric, colonial framing and support for chronologically broad African and diasporic histories, cultures, heritage, and contributions | Focused on Africa; it does not prescribe the rest of the world portfolio |
| Digital Inquiry Group, [Reading Like a Historian](https://www.inquirygroup.org/history-lessons) | Central questions, sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, close reading, multiple perspectives, and evidence-backed claims | Primarily documentary lesson design rather than portfolio coverage |
| UNESCO, [Philosophy, a school of freedom](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/philosophy-school-freedom), and International Baccalaureate, [Philosophy course outline](https://www.ibo.org/programmes/diploma-programme/curriculum/individuals-and-societies/philosophy/) | Philosophy integrated across disciplines and practiced through critical questions and argument; a breadth check spanning being human, ethics, epistemology, religion, science, politics, society, and aesthetics | UNESCO gives method more than content; IB serves older learners and is used only as a comparative breadth check |

## Baseline inventory

The roster contains the stated 90 candidates:

| Draft grouping | Count | Immediate pattern |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Civilizations & Regions | 28 | Wide global reach, but many very long containers centered on states, empires, and political continuity |
| Bounded Stories | 14 | Strong connected-history design; concentrated in exchange systems and transformations after about 1200, especially after 1500 |
| Ideas Across Time | 22 | Broad recurring questions; especially dense around power, institutions, knowledge, exchange, and modern systems |
| Investigations | 26 | Strong evidence-and-uncertainty intent; heavily weighted toward archaeology, monuments, objects, ancient texts, and myth correction |

The draft is much more globally aware than a conventional rulers-and-wars survey. Its best safeguards are already explicit: internal diversity, outward connections, ordinary life, evidence limits, anti-teleology, Indigenous review, and rejection of single-cause collapse stories. The main risk is that several of those safeguards live only in review-gate prose while the titles and central questions still promise institutions, empires, conquest, networks, and famous puzzles.

## Findings

### 1. Geographic breadth is strong, but several absences and compressions are real

World History for Us All says whole-world history should embrace peoples around the globe while organizing them into manageable questions, not attempt an encyclopedia. On that standard, the roster passes breadth but has visible weak cells:

| Area | What the roster currently does | Audit finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Australia | `Pacific Voyagers and Island Worlds` names Sahul crossing among possible anchors, but no candidate centers the continent's more than 60,000 years of diverse and continuing First Nations histories, Country, knowledge, colonization, survival, and modern life | **Clear geographic and evidentiary gap.** The Australian Curriculum's deep-time and continuing-history emphasis shows why a crossing mention is not equivalent to a sustained history |
| Central and southern Africa | West Africa, the Swahili coast, Nile/Horn, and a continent-wide colonization/decolonization arc are present; Great Zimbabwe appears as a corrective Investigation | **Strong compression warning.** Central Africa is effectively absent, and southern Africa is carried mainly by Great Zimbabwe and the continent-wide colonial story |
| North Africa beyond Egypt | Egypt is prominent; Maghreb histories appear only incidentally within Islamic, Mediterranean, Ottoman, African-colonial, or modern Middle Eastern scopes | **Coverage weakness**, not proof that a separate Journey is required |
| Lowland South America and Amazonia | Andes and a very broad Latin American arc are present | **Coverage weakness.** Lowland, forest, riverine, and Amazonian histories are not legible as a sustained focus |
| Arctic and circumpolar worlds | No candidate centers Arctic communities, adaptation, exchange, colonial encounters, or modern sovereignty | **Clear blank cell** at the macroregional level; the editorial response remains open |
| Diasporic and stateless communities | Migration, Atlantic slavery, religion, nationalism, and belonging offer cross-cutting entry points, but few candidates sustain one community across places and regimes | **Structural weakness.** Jewish, Romani, African-diasporic beyond enslavement, and Asian-diasporic histories can be mentioned without ever becoming coherent lived histories |

No framework justifies one Journey per region. It does justify making these omissions explicit in a coverage ledger and deciding whether each is intentionally integrated, deliberately deferred, or genuinely missing.

### 2. The portfolio underrepresents the deep human past and thins sharply near the present

World History for Us All notes that the Paleolithic accounts for roughly 95 percent of our species' history and argues for serious attention before agriculture. The optional roster has investigations about first arrivals, ancient DNA, Göbekli Tepe, and Stonehenge, plus thematic anchors on migration, food, knowledge, and environment. It does **not** have a Story or regional Journey centered on long forager histories, varied social worlds before farming, or how scholars reconstruct them. Because the main timeline may supply orientation, this is not automatically a missing Journey; it is nevertheless a clear depth imbalance in the optional portfolio.

At the other end, `Globalization: One World, Unequal Connections` is the only bounded Story whose primary promise is the post-Cold War world, and it is Reserve tier. Idea Trails mention COVID-19, climate response, platforms, and AI, but most regional stories terminate at independence, the Cold War, or state formation. A complete mature portfolio needs an explicit policy for histories since 1991 and for revision as interpretations and evidence change. Whether that produces new Journeys or later chapters is editorial judgment.

Between those ends, the 14 bounded Stories cluster around the Bronze Age, Afroeurasian networks after antiquity, and Atlantic/industrial/imperial/global transformations after 1500. There is less sustained connected-story treatment of first farming societies, the classical-era Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan worlds before the familiar medieval labels, and local or community-scale histories.

### 3. Ordinary life is promised but structurally subordinate

The roster repeatedly asks for women, households, merchants, sailors, workers, migrants, enslaved people, provincial life, food, health, and non-elites. `The Ottoman World: Empire and Everyday Life`, `How Have Families and Gender Shaped Power?`, `Who Works, Who Benefits, Who Is Free?`, food, cities, health, and the Pompeii and industrial-living-standards Investigations are important strengths.

The imbalance is still measurable in the learning promises. Many regional titles and opening questions center cities, kingdoms, states, empires, rulership, conquest, war, exchange systems, and national formation. Ordinary people are often added in the final “review gate” column rather than made causal actors in the Journey's core question. AHA asks history to convey both the particularity of past lives and the scale of human experience; World History for Us All explicitly includes rich and poor, privileged and unprivileged, slave and free, educated and non-literate people. The roster therefore needs a portfolio-wide actor audit, not one token everyday-life Journey.

The weakest ordinary-life lenses are childhood and aging, disability, rural life outside food production, care and household reproduction, leisure and play, clothing and material self-presentation, emotion and friendship, and art/music/performance as lived practice. **Their absence is evidence of thematic concentration; which of them merits a Journey is editorial judgment.**

### 4. Philosophy is broad in subject matter but needs a stronger integration rule

The roster already contains serious recurring questions: political legitimacy, law and justice, empire, freedom and labor, the good life, knowledge, belief and community, rights and personhood, gender and family, memory, technology, war, health, and environmental obligation. This meets much of the World History for Us All “Humans and Ideas” brief and avoids a purely Greek-to-European sequence.

Two distortions remain:

1. Philosophy is most legible as a set of Idea Trails, while the civilization and connected-story candidates do not consistently state the philosophical problem that their historical actors were living. UNESCO's philosophy guidance favors integration with other disciplines and active questioning, so every relevant Journey should identify its historically situated question and competing reasons—not merely point learners to a separate philosophy shelf.
2. `How Should We Live?` names South Asian, Chinese, Greek, Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions, then compresses “African and later global traditions” into a catch-all. This is a concrete plurality problem. Traditions should be selected and named with equal specificity, situated in institutions and lived practice, and allowed internal disagreement.

As a comparative breadth check, the IB philosophy outline also makes visible weak or implicit domains: being human and human nature, aesthetics, philosophy of science distinct from a general knowledge history, and questions of mind, self, death, and freedom. This is **not** evidence that each needs a Journey. It is evidence that the current philosophy inventory should be tested against more than political and ethical history.

### 5. The Investigation set excels at material evidence but does not yet represent the historical record's full range

The C3 Framework treats written documents, objects, artistic works, oral accounts, modified landscapes, and DNA as historical sources; AHA likewise names artifacts, written evidence, and oral traditions. The roster is unusually strong on objects, sites, archaeology, inscriptions, environmental proxies, manuscripts, provenance, and ancient DNA. It also repeatedly distinguishes observation from inference and later tradition.

Across the 26 Investigation candidates, however, the center of gravity is ancient or premodern archaeology and “mystery corrected” design. Modern documentary inquiries include industrial living standards, the Cuban Missile Crisis, Haitian revolutionary voices, the July Crisis, the Gulf of Tonkin, and Piltdown, but there is no Investigation primarily organized around:

- oral history or living memory as the main evidence tradition rather than corroboration;
- photography, film, broadcast audio, music, posters, or advertising;
- maps, censuses, statistics, or other state-produced categories as constructed evidence;
- a routine community archive, household collection, or labor history rather than a famous mystery or crisis;
- born-digital records, algorithmic curation, deleted records, or platform evidence in recent history.

This is a **clear evidence-modality gap**. It matters because available monuments, royal records, and museum objects otherwise become a hidden selection rule for what deserves an Investigation. Digital Inquiry Group and AHA also make the learner's own evidence-backed argument central; Investigation blueprints should therefore test not only whether learners revise a hypothesis, but whether they source, contextualize, corroborate, explain limitations, and communicate a defensible account.

### 6. African and Indigenous histories too often carry the burden of correcting someone else's myth

The draft takes colonial distortions seriously, but several Investigation promises position African and Indigenous histories chiefly as corrections to outsider error: who built Great Zimbabwe, what the Timbuktu manuscripts “change,” whose Benin Bronzes, whether Maya or Cahokia “vanished,” and the Rapa Nui collapse myth. These are worthy inquiries. In aggregate, however, they can make those histories enter the Library as mysteries, denials, disappearances, or restitution problems, while Greece, Rome, China, and empires receive confident long-form continuity narratives.

UNESCO's General History of Africa guidance calls for African histories, cultures, heritage, diasporas, and contributions across a chronological curriculum—not only reactions to colonial misconceptions. The corrective Investigations should remain possible, but they cannot substitute for African- and Indigenous-centered historical questions that begin from community life, intellectual traditions, political choices, creativity, and continuity.

The title `North America Before European Colonization` is the sharpest instance: its own review gate rejects a frozen “pre-contact” ending, but the learner-facing title defines the entire Journey by the event after it. The contradiction is evidence-based and should be resolved even if the candidate remains.

### 7. Political power and large systems are the strongest thematic concentration

The roster covers every major World History for Us All/AP theme, but not at equal depth. Governance, empire, law, rights, war, revolution, markets, networks, and technology recur across all three non-Investigation groupings. Spiritual and moral life, art and representation, kinship, emotion, childhood, disability, and ordinary material culture are thinner. Environment, food, health, gender/family, religion, and philosophy exist, but most have one broad Trail rather than several regionally grounded Journey promises.

This does not call for numerical parity. It does mean that a geographically global roster could still teach a distorted history in which states and systems cause change while families, communities, belief, creativity, and care merely experience it.

### 8. Several overlap clusters need boundary tests, not automatic merging

Chronos's reuse rule is sound: overlap is not duplication when the question, sequence, evidence, and learner transformation differ. The current roster has at least five clusters where that distinction must be made explicit before approval:

| Cluster | Candidates with substantial shared territory | Boundary question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legitimacy and membership | `Who Should Rule?`; laws and justice; empire and difference; rights/personhood/citizenship; nations and belonging; many regional state arcs | What unique conclusion can the learner reach in each that the others cannot supply? |
| Afroeurasian exchange | Silk Roads; Indian Ocean; Swahili Coast; Central Asia; Islamic Worlds; Mongol Century; exchange and migration Trails | Is each sequence organized around a distinct mechanism and human experience, or are the same route anchors being rearranged? |
| Atlantic empire and freedom | Atlantic World; Age of Revolutions; Ending Slavery?; Caribbean Worlds; Latin American Worlds; imperialism; decolonization; Haiti Investigation | Where does one causal story end and the next begin, and which perspectives change? |
| Information and knowledge | Writing; How Do We Know?; Who Controls the Story?; Who Defines the Past?; measurement/data; Uruk, Indus, Shang, Timbuktu, and Piltdown Investigations | Which path teaches storage/control, epistemology, media power, public memory, quantification, or evidentiary method? |
| Industrial transformation | Industrial World; work/freedom; tools/systems; environment; health; cities; living-standards Investigation; globalization | Does each use industrialization as context for a different question, or repeatedly teach the same causal package? |

A practical acceptance test is: two overlapping Journeys may share many Lessons only if each has a different compelling question, thesis, indispensable ordering logic, evidence mix, synthesis, and “why this changes understanding” statement. If those cannot be written without synonyms, merge or rescope later. That test preserves purposeful reuse without forcing a subject into one universal Lesson.

## Distortion watchlist for candidate review

These are not final disposition decisions.

| Candidate or portfolio element | Watch item |
| --- | --- |
| Six-Journey reference runway | Four of six are centered on Mesopotamia/Egypt/early writing/Uruk. This is efficient for reuse, but it is a highly concentrated first impression if learners see the runway as a miniature statement of world history |
| `China Across Time: Order, Division, and Renewal` | A very long arc can imply a stable civilizational or national subject moving toward the PRC; frontier peoples, changing borders, regional plurality, and contingency must shape the thesis, not remain a review note |
| `South Asia: Cities, Empires, and Argument` and `Latin American Worlds: Conquest, Independence, and Inequality` | Continental-scale compression may turn difference into representative examples; breadth must not substitute for coherent scale and internal plurality |
| `African Worlds: Colonization, Resistance, and Independence` | The title begins African modern history with European colonization even though its review gate says the Journey must begin earlier; UNESCO's warning about Eurocentric framing makes this more than a stylistic concern |
| `The Modern Middle East: Empires, Nations, and Oil` | Oil, war, and outside intervention are consequential but can crowd out social, intellectual, cultural, labor, gender, migration, and ordinary urban histories |
| `North America Before European Colonization` | The title freezes Indigenous history at a colonial boundary that the candidate's own scope says it should cross |
| Investigation portfolio | Archaeological uncertainty and myth correction are overrepresented relative to ordinary archival work, oral history, audiovisual sources, quantitative categories, and recent digital evidence |

## Clear findings versus editorial judgment

| Evidence-based finding | Decision still requiring editorial judgment |
| --- | --- |
| Australia/First Nations deep time is not sustained anywhere in the 90-candidate roster | Add a Journey, broaden and retitle Pacific coverage, or integrate the history through another deliberate structure |
| Central Africa, the Arctic/circumpolar world, the Maghreb beyond Egypt, and lowland/Amazonian South America are blank or weak cells | Which cells merit a Journey, which belong within an existing Journey, and which omissions are intentional |
| The optional portfolio is thin in long pre-farming human history and after 1991 | Whether the main timeline is sufficient, whether existing Journeys extend, and how close to the present Chronos should go |
| Ordinary-life language is often subordinate to state/empire/system promises | Retitle/rescope existing Journeys, add human-scale Journeys, or enforce an actor-and-causality gate across all Journeys |
| Philosophy covers many recurring questions but is not systematically integrated into regional and connected histories | Which questions recur portfolio-wide and whether any additional Idea-focused Journeys are warranted |
| Investigations overconcentrate on archaeology/material evidence and famous contested cases | Which oral, visual, quantitative, community, and digital inquiries should be added or substituted |
| Several African and Indigenous inquiries are framed primarily as myth correction or disappearance | Which existing titles/questions can be reframed and which positive continuity histories require sustained space |
| Five overlap clusters could duplicate content without distinct learning transformations | Merge, split, retain, or rescope only after candidate-level boundary statements are written |

## Recommended decision inputs for the Wayfinder map

Before approving or pruning candidates, create a lightweight coverage ledger with one row per Journey and these fields:

- date span and primary era;
- one to three primary regions plus connected regions;
- spatial scale: local, community, regional, transregional, or global;
- primary and secondary themes;
- focal actors and social locations;
- ordinary-life lens;
- philosophical or recurring human question;
- primary evidence traditions;
- historical reasoning affordance: sourcing, context, corroboration, comparison, causation, continuity/change, contingency, significance, interpretation, argument;
- perspectives centered, contested, and still missing;
- overlap cluster and distinct learner transformation;
- intentional omissions, compression risks, and revision needs.

Then audit empty intersections and monopolies rather than aiming for equal counts. At minimum, examine era × region, region × ordinary-life lens, region × evidence tradition, theme × era, and philosophical question × cultural tradition. A mature portfolio can be selective; it should not be accidentally selective.

## Research conclusion

The 90-candidate roster is a strong starting map, not yet an intentionally complete portfolio. Its clearest strengths are global ambition, connected histories, recurring questions, explicit anti-distortion gates, and evidence-aware Investigations. Its clearest weaknesses are missing or compressed geographies; weak optional depth before farming and after the Cold War; political/imperial/system concentration; ordinary life relegated to safeguards; philosophy insufficiently woven through all Journey types; and an Investigation set dominated by archaeology, famous uncertainty, and myth correction.

The next portfolio decision should not be “add every missing topic.” It should make selection logic explicit, fill or consciously accept the high-confidence gaps, and require each retained Journey to prove a distinct historical and philosophical learning purpose.
