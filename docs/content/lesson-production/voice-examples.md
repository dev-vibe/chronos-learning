# Before and after: voice and question examples

Status: real pairs from Chronos lessons, kept next to the rules they illustrate. The rules live in the [lesson creation runbook](../lesson-creation-runbook.md) (Stage 7 **Voice and storytelling** and **Point-first sections**, Stage 12 for questions). Read the pair for a rule before applying it, and add a pair here when a review catches a new kind of mistake. Each pair names what was wrong in a line; the rule says why.

Most pairs come from the Akkad revision of November 2026, after a homeschooling parent and a cold kid read went through the published lesson.

## Orient first, then state the point plainly

**Before**

> Tar’am-Agade’s father told his own story in stone. Naram-Sin’s victory stele, a carved slab about 2 meters tall, celebrates a win over the Lullubi, a mountain people. …
>
> The stele is a message, not a battle report. It shows how Naram-Sin wanted his power seen, not how any city was governed afterward…
>
> The stone’s later life makes the point sharper. It first stood at Sippar, in Mesopotamia. About a thousand years later, in the 1100s BCE, an Elamite king named Shutruk-Nahhunte carried it off to Susa as war booty… French excavators found it at Susa in 1898. Much of Naram-Sin’s original inscription is lost.

**After**

> Kings also had their wins carved in stone. Look closely at the carving below. It is a stele, a tall carved stone. It was made for Naram-Sin, the princess’s father and a later king of Akkad, to celebrate his win over people who lived in the mountains. …
>
> The carving shows Naram-Sin as more than a man: anyone who stood before the stone saw a king who looked like a god. …
>
> About a thousand years later, another king, from a land to the east, used the same stone to boast about himself. …
>
> The stone shows a king winning a battle. The ovens at Leilan show the work of keeping a city, every single day.

What changed: the opening needed the reader to remember an earlier section and decode a metaphor; the point was stated as a correction; “makes the point sharper” never said how; dates and find spots that served nothing moved to the caption; the first line says why the section is here and the last line ties it back to the lesson's question.

## The opening is a hook and one question

**Before** (a dozen new names and ideas before the question)

> Sargon, king of Agade, wanted everyone to know how far his reach went. One of his inscriptions boasts that boats from Meluhha, Magan and Dilmun, lands that scholars identify with the Indus cities, Oman and Bahrain, were tied up at the quay of Agade, his capital. …
>
> Agade stood somewhere in Mesopotamia, the land around the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in present-day Iraq. Between about 2350 and 2150 BCE, Sargon and later kings such as Naram-Sin claimed power over cities that had long ruled themselves. When one center claims power over many other communities, historians call it an empire. …

**After**

> Sargon was king of Akkad, a kingdom in what is now Iraq. His scribes wrote that boats from lands far across the sea tied up at the docks of his capital city. Archaeologists would love to stand on those docks, but nobody has ever found the city. What we know about how Akkad’s kings ruled comes from other cities. …
>
> Winning a city took one battle. Keeping it was the hard part. After the army went home, what would a king need so that the people there kept doing what he said? Make a guess before you read on.

What changed: the hook stays; names the point does not need (Meluhha, Magan, Dilmun, the rivers, Naram-Sin) go to the section that needs them or nowhere; the question comes in one plain sentence, with a guess the ending pays off. (The guess is now asked with the guess module, not in the prose: see the next pair.)

## Ask for the guess with the guess module, not in the prose

**Before** (the guess is a request in the text, easy to read past)

> Winning a city could take a single battle. Keeping it was the hard part. After the army went home, what would a king need so that the people there kept doing what he said? Make a guess before you read on.

**After** (the prose ends on the hook; the question is a `guess` module, the last thing in the opening section)

> Winning a city could take a single battle. Keeping it was the hard part.
>
> **[Guess before reading]** After the army went home, what would a king need so that the people there kept doing what he said?
> `[ A king would need… ]` **Save my guess**
>
> *(The rest of the lesson appears once the guess is saved. At the closing paragraph, “So what did it take to keep a city? …”, the learner sees “You guessed: …” in their own words.)*

What changed: a guess helps a learner remember the answer only if they actually make one, and “Make a guess before you read on” asks for one without waiting for it. The guess module waits, keeps the question to one plain sentence, gives a sentence starter, and pays the guess off where the ending answers it. The question is not repeated in the prose above the box.

## Name budget and look-alike names

**Before**

> Sargon, king of Agade… The impressions named Tar’am-Agade, daughter of Naram-Sin, king of Akkad.

**After**

> Sargon was king of Akkad, a kingdom in what is now Iraq… the docks of his capital city… a princess from Akkad, the daughter of a later king of Akkad… another king, from a land to the east…

What changed: Akkad, Agade, Akkadian and Tar’am-Agade share a root, and readers lost track of which was which. The kingdom is “Akkad” everywhere and is said to be a kingdom; “Akkad’s” replaces “Akkadian”; the capital, the princess, the defeated people and the second king are described instead of named. (An early draft put their names in term boxes on the descriptions; readers found it odd to tap “a princess from Akkad” and get a name they had never seen, so those boxes went too.) Uruk and “Tell” Leilan went too. The names pass counted 13 names before and about 5 after.

## Every detail serves the point (no seductive details)

**Before**

> In August 1999, on what was meant to be the last day of digging in one part of the royal palace at Urkesh, excavators unexpectedly began finding inscribed lumps of clay. They kept digging. The impressions named Tar’am-Agade…

**After**

> Not far from Leilan, Akkad’s kings dealt with the city of Urkesh in a different way. Urkesh kept its own kings, and a princess from Akkad, the daughter of a later king of Akkad, turns up inside their palace.

What changed: the dig story is vivid, and kid readers remembered it, but a parent could not see why the princess was in the lesson at all. Urkesh is there as the second way of keeping a city, so the section now opens by setting it against Leilan, and the dig date and seal size are gone.

## Say what happened, not what didn't

**Before**

> Either way, her seal shows a royal family tie at work inside the palace. It does not show that Urkesh was run the way Leilan was.

**After**

> So Akkad’s kings dealt with different cities in different ways. Leilan got an official from Akkad, who ran big ovens to feed its workers. Urkesh got a princess.

## Plain words, not invented phrases

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| What they say is loud. | They make big claims. |
| The stone kept boasting long after Naram-Sin was gone. | About a thousand years later, another king used the same stone to boast about himself. |
| Akkad’s kings reached into different cities in different ways. | Akkad’s kings dealt with different cities in different ways. |
| found what staying in charge looked like on the ground | found what keeping a city really took |
| Holding Leilan was daily work, done by many hands. | Keeping Leilan took work every day from many people. |
| One of his boasts, written down by his scribes, says… (and “boast” seven more times) | His scribes wrote that… / Sargon’s records… (a loaded word repeated until it reads as a judgment of the man; we cannot know his motives, so the lesson describes what the records say and notes once that they are his side of the story) |
| Keeping it took people on the spot… | Keeping it took people from Akkad living in the city… |
| The ovens at Leilan tell the other half of the story. / This is winning as the stone shows it. | The stone shows a king winning a battle. The ovens at Leilan show the work of keeping a city, every single day. |

## Say the plain thing

**Before:** “Her seal was rolled across clay that closed a door in the palace.” (The owner, an adult, could not tell what this meant.)

**After:** “Palace doors there were tied shut with a rope, and a lump of clay was pressed over the rope. Archaeologists found lumps of that clay rolled with the princess’s cylinder seal.”

## State the main idea; ask a question the lesson does not answer for them

**Before** (the lesson already said “At Leilan, staying in charge meant putting an Akkadian official over the city and feeding its workers, every single day.”)

> Sargon’s army could win a city in a single battle. Why was staying in charge of a city like Tell Leilan so much more work?

**Also before** (no sentence to copy, but no evidence to answer from: the lesson never says how either city fared)

> Leilan got an official from Akkad and big ovens to feed its workers. Urkesh got a princess from Akkad. Which way do you think worked better for keeping a city?

**After**

> At Leilan, Akkad put its own official in charge. At Urkesh, a princess from Akkad joined the palace of the city’s own king. If you were Akkad’s king, which would you choose for a new city? Give one reason from the lesson.

What changed: the first question could be answered by copying a sentence; the second asked for a judgment the lesson gives no evidence for. The lesson still states the main idea plainly; the question asks the learner to use what it teaches about each city to make a choice.

## A real question, not an exam stem

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| At Leilan, archaeologists found a fortified administrative building… At Urkesh, they found seal impressions… What is the strongest conclusion? (best answer: “Both places had Akkadian connections, but the evidence points to different or uncertain arrangements.”) | At Leilan, an official sent by Akkad ran the farm work. What was different at Urkesh? (best answer: “A princess from Akkad used her seal in the palace.”) |
