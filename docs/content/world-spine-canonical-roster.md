# Canonical World Spine roster

Status: **Approved on July 19, 2026; product-owner amendments incorporated on July 19, 2026.** This canonical curriculum document does not itself change runtime fixtures or application behavior.

## Decision summary

The approved World Spine contains **185 lessons in 12 chapters**. The count is the result of applying the selection rubric to the whole sequence, not a preset allocation. Every node must provide a distinct causal, evidentiary, or explanatory handoff suitable for ages 10–14. Geographic breadth is reviewed as a diagnostic, never a quota.

The complete machine-checkable roster—including numeric dates, full scopes, rationales, prerequisites, predecessor/successor links, aliases, research status, and readiness—is in [data/world-spine-canonical-roster.csv](data/world-spine-canonical-roster.csv). The ordered tables below repeat those fields in readable form.

## Readiness definitions

- **Approved reference**: an implemented lesson already accepted as the product reference.
- **Ready candidate**: the node decision and source base are sufficient to enter a lesson brief after approval.
- **Planned**: included in the canonical order, but its source/claim brief has not been completed.
- **Research required**: a material date, framing, synthesis, or recent-history judgment needs dedicated review before briefing.
- **Blocked**: inclusion is approved but external evidence, rights, or prerequisite work prevents production. No proposed node is currently marked Blocked.

## Intellectual and dialectical continuity rule

Intellectual history is a causal thread, not enrichment attached to political events. A sequence should show:

1. the inherited question, text, practice, or authority;
2. competing answers and the methods used to defend them;
3. the schools, courts, monasteries, academies, examination systems, universities, or communities that preserved the argument;
4. practical effects on law, ethics, government, social belonging, science, or education;
5. later transmission, synthesis, criticism, or rejection.

A thinker may earn a required node when the intervention marks an intelligible turn in that chain. Biographical fame alone is insufficient, but compressing a durable dialectical transformation merely because several famous people participate is also a curriculum failure. The rule applies consistently to Greek, Chinese, South Asian, Iranian and Persianate, Jewish, Christian, Islamic, and other intellectual traditions.

## Slavery and abolition framing

The Spine must make four connected conclusions visible rather than leaving them to implication:

1. Slavery, captive labor, and related systems of coercive dependency appeared in many societies across Africa, Asia, Europe, the Mediterranean, the Islamic worlds, and the Americas. Their legal forms and lived conditions differed; no civilization, religion, race, or region has a monopoly on the history.
2. Atlantic racial slavery was not history's first or only slavery, but its oceanic scale, hereditary racialization, plantation commodity production, and integration with European imperial and financial power require distinct treatment.
3. The rise of sustained abolitionism as a mass moral and political project in the Atlantic world owed exceptional institutional force to Western Quaker and evangelical Protestant networks. Their role must be taught alongside Black abolitionists and testimony, free Black communities, women organizers, other Christian and non-Christian allies, natural-rights arguments, enslaved resistance, and revolutions—while acknowledging that other Christians defended slavery.
4. Legal abolition spread through organizing, revolt, revolution, legislation, civil war, diplomacy, naval enforcement, imperial power, and locally specific African, Asian, Middle Eastern, European, and American processes. Emancipation did not immediately produce equality or eliminate coerced labor.

This is a causal standard, not a credit-allocation exercise: learners should understand both the unusual historical force of the Protestant-led Atlantic abolition movement and why it was neither solitary nor sufficient by itself.

## Sequence review

### Gaps and transitions

Within each chapter, nodes are ordered by the beginning of their stated range; ties retain causal teaching order. Chapter boundaries can overlap when a long-duration process supplies the handoff to the next period.

- **Deep time to farming:** the opening chapter restores human origins, mobility, and multiple agricultural beginnings that the legacy timeline omitted. These nodes are prerequisites, not a quota-driven prelude.
- **Cities to states:** Uruk and early writing remain the approved anchor, followed by contrasting Egyptian, Indus, Andean, Nubian, Mesopotamian, steppe, and Chinese pathways. Technology is taught through systems and exchange rather than lone inventors.
- **Bronze to Iron Age:** the roster replaces a sudden “Sea Peoples” collapse with an evidence-aware regional transformation, then shows how iron use, alphabets, and new states developed unevenly.
- **Classical to post-classical:** Rome is one regional thread among Persian, South Asian, East Asian, African, and American systems. The western Roman political breakup is not treated as a global civilizational fall.
- **1000–1450:** Song commercialization, Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan exchange, Mongol conquest, plague, American cities, and Pacific voyaging prevent an abrupt jump from medieval Europe to Columbus.
- **Oceanic contact to industry:** conquest, Columbian exchange, slavery, silver, and state finance make the causal bridge to Atlantic revolutions and industrialization explicit.
- **Industry to world war:** energy, corporate scale, communication, mass politics, high imperialism, and resistance precede the First World War causes node.
- **1945 to present:** decolonization and Cold War rivalry organize the chapter; rights, food, environment, digital networks, health, and globalization carry distinct causal threads to current history.

### Intellectual-history handoffs

- Greek epic, law, and public argument lead to Socratic and Platonic inquiry, Aristotelian system-building, and Hellenistic schools; later nodes carry those arguments through Roman, Jewish, Christian, Islamicate, scholastic, Renaissance, scientific, and Enlightenment transformations.
- Vedic and Upanishadic questions meet Buddhist, Jain, materialist, and other challenges; classical Indian debate institutions, logic, and devotional movements show continuing revision rather than a one-period “Axial Age.”
- Chinese competing schools lead to Qin choices, Han canon and state synthesis, Song-Ming Neo-Confucian renewal, and modern reformers disputing the inheritance.
- Rabbinic interpretation, Islamic debates over reason and revelation, Persianate philosophy and poetry, and medieval Jewish and Christian schools are taught as interacting but non-identical traditions.

### Concentrations and meaningful absences

The ancient and modern portions are denser because later nodes depend on several institutional systems established there and because the surviving evidence supports discrete, teachable causal handoffs. Europe is concentrated from 1450–1945 because Atlantic expansion, industrialization, imperial competition, and the world wars exerted unusually large global consequences—not because European experience is a default measure of importance. The sequence deliberately keeps substantial African, Asian, American, Pacific, and mobile-society histories where they explain their own regions or reshape wider networks.

Not every durable culture, polity, artwork, ruler, battle, or invention can be a required core lesson. Narrow but valuable material moves to Story Arcs, Idea Trails, Investigations, optional lessons, or archive review in the audit. Absence from this roster is not a judgment of worth. Australia before European contact is currently represented through Sahul and Pacific histories but lacks a later, evidence-rich core handoff; this is an explicit absence to revisit if a node with unique causal contribution is proposed. Central Asia appears most often through steppe, Silk Road, and Mongol networks rather than state-by-state survey. Precolonial North America is represented by the peopling and Mississippian-city nodes; regional depth belongs in optional paths unless it changes a core causal handoff.

### Themes checked across the sequence

- food systems, environment, and disease;
- cities, states, law, empire, resistance, and political legitimacy;
- coerced labor, slavery, migration, and rights;
- writing, print, science, media, and historical evidence;
- overland, oceanic, commercial, religious, and technological exchange;
- continuity and adaptation alongside conquest and collapse.

## Approved decisions and research gates

These are not hidden homework for the approver. Each item records a genuine scholarly or editorial risk, the recommended resolution, and what approval would authorize.

1. **Zoroastrian chronology — recommend keep, research-gated.** Zarathustra is consequential, but proposed dates vary widely and the surviving texts are later. Keep the required node, avoid a falsely precise biography, teach the date problem, and require a dedicated source brief before production.
2. **Israel and Judah — recommend keep with an evidence/tradition protocol.** Teach archaeological remains, external inscriptions, biblical texts, and later religious interpretation as different evidence classes. Approval endorses this method; it does not ask the curriculum to affirm or reject a faith claim.
3. **Israel–Palestine, 1947–49 — recommend keep with terminology and sensitivity review.** The node should cover UN partition, Israel’s establishment and war of independence, regional war, Palestinian displacement and the Nakba, and divergent memories. No single community’s preferred label should silently control the whole account.
4. **Bantu-language dispersals — recommend keep one long synthesis, positioned by its early phase.** The history spans many centuries and was neither one migration wave nor a simple package of language, iron, and farming. Use later callbacks in the Swahili and Great Zimbabwe nodes; split only if research shows two age-suitable causal lessons are clearer.
5. **Two Atlantic-slavery nodes — approved, with a global-history and abolition-causality requirement.** The first situates Atlantic slavery within the worldwide history of enslavement, then explains its distinctive political economy, scale, racialization, forced transport, and plantation demand. The second explains operation and lived consequence: the Middle Passage, plantation regimes, diaspora, survival, resistance, and revolt. The later abolition node must make the exceptional organizing force of Western Quaker and evangelical Protestant networks clear while also teaching Black abolitionism and testimony, enslaved resistance and revolution, allied reform traditions, and the state power required to enact and enforce emancipation.
6. **AI versus historical method at the endpoint — recommend end with historical method.** Keep platforms, digital divides, and AI provisionally research-gated because their historical significance is still developing. The final required node should be **New Evidence Changes How We Know the Past**, returning learners to evidence, revision, uncertainty, and historical judgment.

## Canonical ID policy

IDs are semantic, lowercase, dot-delimited, and do not encode mutable roster position. Existing canonical IDs are preserved for Farming and Settlements, Uruk, and early writing. Legacy stub IDs are aliases only; aliases never determine order. Merged aliases point to the one canonical concept that subsumes them. A future split must create new IDs rather than changing the meaning of an approved ID.
